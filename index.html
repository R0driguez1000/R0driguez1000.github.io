Overview

The lens is a crystalline object that sits behind your iris and is responsible for precisely focusing light onto the retina. A cataract is a clouding of that lens that occurs when, among other reasons, age related changes to the lens proteins called crystallins which bind to each other and to the surrounding cells in a way that scatter incoming light rather than transmitting it[1]. Cataracts remain the current worldwide leading cause of preventable blindness, and due to its heavy correlation with age, as the average lifespan has increased, so too has the burden of this disease[2].

Cataracts come in multiple different forms. They are usually sorted into three groups depending on where in the lens they happen to form. The first is nuclear, forming in the central area, and causing a gradual yellowing and blurring of vision. The second is cortical, which are wedge shaped and often cause glare, and finally posterior subcapsular, which forms under the back capsule and generally affects near vision and reading first[22][23]. Cortical cataracts are especially noteworthy; a three-year study of adults aged 65-74 found that they occur at roughly 3 times the rate of the other types during that period[22].

Once diagnosed most cataracts are monitored via a regular review with an optometrist, where options can be discussed such as the need for glasses or surgery.

What factors increase the risk of cataracts?

There are multiple factors that can increase an individual's risk of developing cataracts:

  • Genetics — twin studies estimate that close to half of the variation in nuclear cataract severity, as well as over half of cortical cataract risk stems from inherited genetic factors rather than environment alone[12][13].
  • Ultraviolet light/occupational exposure — a review of occupational studies found a strong correlation between UV radiation and cataract development, with evidence also suggesting a correlation between other occupational exposures, such as welding fumes and ionising radiation[3]. Field of view is the dominant factor for determining how much ultraviolet light reaches the lens, making sunglasses with an adequate UV-A protection more important than lens darkness alone[4].
  • Diabetes — associated with approximately a doubling in the detection rate of cataracts, with highest relative risk being young people as well as those with long standing diabetes[5][6].
  • Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islanders experience a 3 times higher rate of vision loss than non-Indigenous Australians, with cataracts among the leading causes[8].
  • Smoking — smoking has been found to lead to an increased risk of age related cataract, especially current smoking. It particularly is associated with the nuclear form of cataract[10][11].

The single biggest factor in developing a cataract remains age, as the process of protein aggregation that leads to a change in the lens happens in every ageing lens to some degree given enough time[1].

Management/Treatment

There is currently no medical treatment that is able to prevent a cataract from occurring or reverse one when it has already happened. The only definitive treatment option is surgery, which involves removing the cloudy lens and replacing it with a clear, artificial one[23]. This type of surgery is extensively practiced worldwide, with over a quarter of a million patients represented in a recent review of surgical timing approaches alone[18].

Modern surgical options

Modern surgical options vary. A large, randomised trial comparing femtosecond laser-assisted surgery against the standard option of phacoemulsification, where the surgeon uses ultrasound energy to break the clouded lens into small pieces before removing it, found the two different techniques to be equally safe and effective at three months, across the criteria of vision, patient reported health and safety outcomes[14]. New options like this are available, though they should be seen as an alternative that shares similar results and not as necessarily a superior option.

Surgical intervention has been shown to lead to consistent improvements in quality of life. One study following patients before and after cataract surgery found significant improvement in social function, general health perception, and visual acuity[15]. A separate study found similarly promising improvements in visual function following phacoemulsification[16].

Timing, self-care, and waiting for surgery

One key factor to consider is the time it takes to get surgery. Delaying it can have a much more severe impact than many realize. Older adults with cataracts have a measurable higher rate of falls and fall related injuries while awaiting surgery[19]. However evidence on whether or not surgery leads to an immediate reduction in fall risk afterwards is mixed. One study found that despite falling less overall, a group that got surgery had similar risk profiles when accounting for other factors[20]. While awaiting treatment there are tools such as portable electronic magnifiers which have been shown to meaningfully help with near-vision tasks such as reading, on top of standard optical magnifiers[21], and things like anti-glare coatings or UV protective glasses can help to reduce the strain without worsening the cataract.

When to seek urgent care

Cataracts do not cause pain and progress slowly, as such they typically do not warrant urgent or emergency care. However if you experience symptoms like sudden vision loss, eye pain, redness or flashes of light, you should schedule an urgent meeting with your GP or optometrist, as these are not standard cataract symptoms.

FAQ

Can my cataract come back after surgery? +

Since the natural lens is replaced by an artificial lens, the breakdown and clumping of proteins that causes cataracts cannot take place, nor can the natural lens regrow itself. A small minority of patients develop a clouding of the lens capsule left behind, but this can be treated with a quick laser procedure and does not necessitate another surgery[17].

Is cataract surgery safe? +

Cataract surgery remains one of the most routine and common surgeries in the world, with modern surgeries having a reported success rate about 97%, and comparisons of different techniques finding comparably safe outcomes between them[23][14].

What are the initial symptoms of a cataract? +

Initial symptoms can depend on the type of cataract. Common symptoms include a yellowing of vision, increased glare and blurry vision.

Do I need surgery on both eyes at the same time? +

This is largely up to preference. A Cochrane review found that there was no clinically significant difference in outcomes between patients who had surgery done on both eyes at the same time, or on one eye at a time in a delayed sequence, however the certainty of that evidence was rated low[18].

Will surgery actually make a difference in my day-to-day life? +

Studies have found real measurable improvement in not just visual acuity for patients who have undergone cataract surgery, but also their reported ability to perform commonplace physical and social activities[15][16].

Who Can Help

If you are suffering from or developing cataracts there will likely be a number of health professionals involved in supporting you, and to whom you can go for help.

First point of contact

Optometrist

Optometrists are the primary eye healthcare workers and will commonly be the ones to diagnose a cataract and can teach you about prevention and management.

Surgical care

Ophthalmologist

Ophthalmologists are medical specialists who will be the ones performing any surgeries and managing complications that arise from them.

General health

GP

A GP can be involved to refer, as well as help manage related conditions such as diabetes.

Daily living support

Low-vision specialist

Can help you with rehabilitation following surgery, or magnification aids while you wait for/recover from it.[21]

In the case of symptoms that are serious enough to be considered an emergency such as sudden loss of vision, eye pain or flashes of bright light, you should make an emergency booking with an optometrist as soon as possible, as they are equipped with the knowledge and especially the unique equipment required to reach a diagnosis. If the symptoms are not just ocular, then consider an emergency booking with a GP or ophthalmologist.
